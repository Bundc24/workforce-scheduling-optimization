Front Desk Scheduler

A tool that uses math to build a fair weekly schedule for a learning center front desk, and to help decide which new people to hire.

1. The story

A learning center has a front desk. It is open Monday to Thursday 9am to 5pm, and Friday 9am to 3pm. Exactly one student worker sits at the desk at a time. We chop the week into half-hour blocks, so there are 76 blocks (38 hours) to fill.

Each worker fills out a sheet before the semester:

They writeMeaning
Ideal hoursHow many hours a week they would like to work.
Max hoursA limit. They must never be scheduled above it.
PPreferred. They can work this block and they would like to.
A or blankAvailable. They can work it, but it is not their favorite.
Class (red)Busy. They cannot work this block.

A manager used to solve this by hand. With 8 or more workers and 76 blocks, the number of possible schedules is astronomically large, so this project asks a computer to search them all smartly. The method is called mixed-integer linear optimization (MILP), solved with the Gurobi solver.

Two jobs:

Part 1, Scheduling: given a list of workers, build the best weekly schedule.
Part 2, Hiring: if some blocks cannot be covered by returning workers, or we might want more people, work out which applicants to hire.

3. Part 1: Building the schedule

3.1 The facts we feed in (the data)

SymbolMeaning
sA person. “Sam” is one s.
tOne half-hour block of the week (76 of them). day(t) is its weekday.
a[s,t]1 if person s can work block t (P, A or blank), otherwise 0.
p[s,t]1 if person s marked block t as Preferred.
I_s and M_sIdeal and max weekly hours of person s.
δ = 0.5Hours in one block.
L_min = 4, L_max = 10Shortest and longest shift, in blocks (2 hours and 5 hours).
H_sSmallest allowed weekly hours: 2 for a returning person; for a hired applicant, 4 (or their max if it is lower).

3.2 Decision variables (the quantities the model solves for)

VariableTypeIn words
x[s,t]yes/noPerson s works block t. This is the schedule itself.
y[s,t]yes/noA shift starts for person s at block t. A helper that lets us count shifts.
g[t]yes/noBlock t is a gap: nobody is at the desk.
u[s]number ≥ 0Hours person s is under their ideal.
o[s]number ≥ 0Hours person s is over their ideal (but within max).
wnumber ≥ 0The worst shortfall (as a fraction of ideal) of anyone.

Weekly hours are not a variable. They are a formula: h_s = 0.5 × (number of blocks person s works).

If someone is busy in a block, the model has no variable for it, so that assignment cannot happen. “Never when busy” is built into the variables instead of being a separate rule.

3.3 The objective (the score; smaller is better)

minimize 1000 · (gaps) + 150 · w + 15 · (total shortfall, as fractions of ideal) + 2 · (total overage, as fractions of ideal) − 1 · (number of preferred blocks given)
TermWhy it is there
Gaps × 1000Biggest price. Leaving the desk empty is the worst outcome, so a gap only appears when nobody legally can cover it.
w × 150Pushes up the person who is worst off, so the shortfall is spread evenly, not dumped on one person.
Shortfall × 15Fewer total missed hours is better. Measured as a fraction (6 wanted, 4 given = 33% short; 20 wanted, 18 given = 10% short).
Overage × 2Between ideal and max is fine, so this price is tiny. It only stops extra hours piling on one person for no reason.
Preferred × −1Each P block given lowers the score (a reward). It is the tie-breaker among otherwise equal schedules.
The model adds up a penalty score for every candidate schedule and looks for the lowest one. Big penalties (gaps) matter most; small ones (preferences) break ties.

3.4 Constraints (the rules)

1. Coverage hard rule

for every block t: Σ_s x[s,t] + g[t] = 1

Exactly one person works the block, or it is marked a gap.

One chair at the desk. Either one person sits there, or we write “nobody can”. The gap switch stops the whole puzzle from breaking just because a block has nobody free.

2. Never above max hard rule

for every person s: h_s ≤ M_s
Nobody works more hours than they said they could.

3. Everyone works a little hard rule

for every person s: h_s ≥ H_s

A returning person gets at least 2 hours. Together with the shift rules, that means at least one real shift.

Nobody on the team gets left out with zero hours.

4. Spot where a shift starts hard rule

y[s,t] ≥ x[s,t] − x[s,t−1] y[s,t] ≤ x[s,t] y[s,t] ≤ 1 − x[s,t−1]

(For the first block of a day, treat “the block before” as 0.) Together these force y = 1 exactly when person s works block t but did not work the block before.

A start is “the light just switched on”. Working now and not working a moment ago means a new shift began. This helper lets rules 5 and 6 count shifts.

5. No tiny shifts: at least 2 hours hard rule

y[s,t] ≤ x[s,t+1], y[s,t] ≤ x[s,t+2], y[s,t] ≤ x[s,t+3]

If a shift starts at block t, the next three blocks must be worked too, so the shift is at least 4 blocks (2 hours). If fewer than 4 blocks remain in the day (late afternoon, or after Friday’s 3pm close), a shift cannot start there at all.

Nobody gets asked to come in for just 30 minutes.

6. One shift per day hard rule

for every person s and day d: Σ_(t on day d) y[s,t] ≤ 1

The number of starts equals the number of separate blocks of work, so at most one block per day: no split shifts. It says “at most”, so working zero shifts that day is allowed. Nobody has to work every day.

If Sam comes in Tuesday, it is one stretch, not 9 to 12 and then back again at 3.

7. Not too long: at most 5 hours hard rule

for every person s and day d: Σ_(t on day d) x[s,t] ≤ 10

With one block per day, this caps that block at 10 half-hours.

8. Measuring distance from ideal bookkeeping

for every person s: h_s − I_s = o[s] − u[s]

This splits “hours minus ideal” into an over part and an under part. Both are penalized in the objective, so only one of them can be above zero at the best answer. (If both were positive, lowering both would keep the equation true and lower the penalty.)

If you wanted 10 hours and got 8, then u = 2 and o = 0. If you got 12, then u = 0 and o = 2.

9. Fairness link bookkeeping

for every person s: u[s] / max(I_s, 1) ≤ w

The variable w must be at least as large as everyone’s shortfall fraction. The objective then tries to make w small, which means lifting the worst-off person. This is the min-max trick from linear optimization.

It is like raising the lowest test score in a class instead of padding the highest one.

3.5 Final formulation (Part 1)

Scheduling model for a given team \(S\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{x,\,y,\,g,\,u,\,o,\,w}\quad & W_{\text{gap}}\sum_{t\in T} g_t \;+\; W_{\text{fair}}\,w \;+\; W_{\text{sum}}\sum_{s\in S}\frac{u_s}{I'_s} \;+\; W_{\text{over}}\sum_{s\in S}\frac{o_s}{I'_s} \;-\; W_{\text{pref}}\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{t\in T} p_{st}\,x_{st}\\[4pt] \text{subject to}\quad & \sum_{s\in S} x_{st} + g_t = 1 && \forall\, t\in T && \text{(C1)}\\ & \delta\sum_{t\in T} x_{st} \le M_s && \forall\, s\in S && \text{(C2)}\\ & \delta\sum_{t\in T} x_{st} \ge H_s && \forall\, s\in S && \text{(C3)}\\ & y_{st} \ge x_{st}-x_{s,t-1},\;\; y_{st}\le x_{st},\;\; y_{st}\le 1-x_{s,t-1} && \forall\, s\in S,\ t\in T && \text{(C4)}\\ & y_{st} \le x_{s,t+j},\quad j=1,2,3 && \forall\, s\in S,\ t\in T && \text{(C5)}\\ & \sum_{t\in T_d} y_{st} \le 1 && \forall\, s\in S,\ d\in D && \text{(C6)}\\ & \sum_{t\in T_d} x_{st} \le L_{\max} && \forall\, s\in S,\ d\in D && \text{(C7)}\\ & \delta\sum_{t\in T} x_{st} - I_s = o_s - u_s && \forall\, s\in S && \text{(C8)}\\ & \frac{u_s}{I'_s} \le w && \forall\, s\in S && \text{(C9)}\\ & x_{st},\,y_{st},\,g_t \in\{0,1\};\quad u_s,\,o_s,\,w \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

Conventions: \(x_{s,t-1}=0\) for the first block of each day. If fewer than 3 blocks remain in the day after block \(t\), (C5) is replaced by \(y_{st}=0\). \(x_{st}\) and \(y_{st}\) exist only where \(a_{st}=1\) and are treated as 0 elsewhere. \(I'_s=\max(I_s,1)\), \(L_{\max}=10\), \(\delta=0.5\).

4. Part 2: Deciding who to hire

4.1 The problem

Sometimes a block cannot be covered by anyone on the returning team. Then we need to hire, and we need someone who can fill that gap. Sometimes there are no gaps, but we still might want more people. There is a catch: the desk only has 38 hours. Every new hire takes hours from someone already on the team. So the question is not “who can fill the gap?”, it is “how many people, and which ones, so that everyone ends up close to their ideal hours?”

New applicants fill out the same sheet (ideal, max, P / A / Class) in a second Excel file.

4.2 The approach: try every possible team

Let R be the returning staff and A′ the applicants who could be hired. For each possible group of hires H (no one, just Elena, Elena + Ravi, and so on, up to 3 hires) the tool:

1. Builds a team R ∪ H and runs the whole Part 1 model on it.
2. Writes down a scorecard for the resulting schedule.
3. Ranks all the scorecards and shows the full schedule of the winner.

Every applicant is treated with the exact same rules, weights and fairness as a returning person. A hired applicant gets at least 4 hours (a flat floor so nobody is hired for a 2-hour job), never more than their max, and is pushed toward their ideal just like everyone else.

Imagine picking a soccer team. We try every possible lineup, play a practice game with each, check how happy every player is, and pick the lineup that leaves everyone happiest.

4.3 The scorecard (one row per team)

ColumnWhat it tells the manager
Gap slotsHow many blocks nobody covers.
Worst shortfall %How far below ideal the worst-off person (returner or new hire) ends up.
Returner shortfall (hrs)Total hours returners are below their ideal.
New-hire shortfall (hrs)Same, for the hired applicants.
Total shortfall (hrs)Those two added up.
Returner hrs vs no-hireHours returners gained or lost compared to hiring nobody. A negative number means the hire took hours from them.
Preferred % of desk hrsHow much of the week is staffed during blocks people marked P.
FlagsA warning if a returner who lost hours is now under 75% of ideal, or a new hire is under 75% of ideal.

4.4 How teams are ranked

sort by: (1) fewest gaps (2) smallest worst-off shortfall (rounded to whole %) (3) smallest total shortfall (4) fewest hires

Rule 4 is how “hire 1, not 2” happens. A second hire only wins if it makes the shortfall smaller. The tool shows the full schedule for rank 1 automatically, and show(k) shows any other row. The manager can read the table and overrule the ranking.

4.5 Final formulation (what the code runs)

Enumerate teams, solve Part 1 for each, then rank

For every hiring set \(H\subseteq A'\) with \(|H|\le K\):

\[ \mathcal{P}(H):\quad \text{solve the Part 1 model with } S = R\cup H,\quad H_s=\begin{cases}2 & s\in R\\ \min(4,\,M_s) & s\in H\end{cases} \]

which returns hours \(h_s(H)\) and gaps \(G(H)=\sum_t g_t\). Then choose

\[ H^{*}\in\operatorname*{lexmin}_{H\subseteq A',\ |H|\le K}\Big(\,G(H),\ \operatorname{round}\!\big(100\,w(H)\big),\ \sigma(H),\ |H|\,\Big) \] \[ w(H)=\max_{s\in R\cup H}\frac{\max\{0,\,I_s-h_s(H)\}}{I'_s},\qquad \sigma(H)=\sum_{s\in R\cup H}\max\{0,\,I_s-h_s(H)\} \]

“lexmin” compares the four numbers in order: gaps first, then the worst-off shortfall, then total shortfall, then number of hires. A set whose model is infeasible is dropped.

4.6 Hiring as a single MILP (for the math-minded)

You can write hiring as one MILP by adding a yes/no switch \(z_a\) for each applicant \(a\in A\) (\(z_a=1\) means hired), tying an applicant’s work to that switch with big-M style links, and adding a price per hire \(W_{\text{hire}}\) (between the gap price and the fairness price).

Single-model formulation, team \(S=R\cup A\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{x,y,g,u,o,w,z}\quad & W_{\text{gap}}\sum_{t} g_t + W_{\text{hire}}\sum_{a\in A} z_a + W_{\text{fair}}\,w + W_{\text{sum}}\sum_{s\in S}\frac{u_s}{I'_s} + W_{\text{over}}\sum_{s\in S}\frac{o_s}{I'_s} - W_{\text{pref}}\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{t} p_{st}x_{st}\\[4pt] \text{subject to}\quad & \text{(C1), (C4)--(C7), (C9)} && \text{as in Part 1, for all } s\in S\\ & \delta\sum_{t} x_{st}\le M_s,\quad \delta\sum_{t} x_{st}\ge H_s,\quad \delta\sum_{t} x_{st}-I_s=o_s-u_s && \forall\, s\in R\\ & x_{at}\le z_a && \forall\, a\in A,\ t\in T\\ & \delta\sum_{t} x_{at}\le M_a\,z_a && \forall\, a\in A\\ & \delta\sum_{t} x_{at}\ge H_a\,z_a && \forall\, a\in A\\ & \delta\sum_{t} x_{at}-I_a\,z_a=o_a-u_a && \forall\, a\in A\\ & \sum_{a\in A} z_a\le K\\ & x,\,y,\,g,\,z\in\{0,1\};\quad u,\,o,\,w\ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

The \(M_a z_a\) rows are the big-M switch: if \(z_a=0\) the applicant has 0 hours; if \(z_a=1\) they may work up to \(M_a\). The \(I_a z_a\) term means ideal hours only count for hired applicants.

Big-M is a light switch. M_a · hire[a] is “you may work up to M hours if the switch is on, and zero if it is off.”

Why the code enumerates instead: the single formula needs a made-up “price per hire”, and the answer would change with that number. Trying every team and showing the table makes the trade-off visible, and the manager decides. Each team’s schedule is still solved exactly as a MILP.

5. Safety checks before solving

Before any math runs, the tool reads every Excel sheet and prints a report. If it finds a real problem, it stops and lists everything to fix, then you re-run.

7. Glossary

OptimizationFinding the best choice among many, according to a score, while obeying rules.
MILPMixed-integer linear program. Some variables are whole-number or yes/no, and every rule and the score are straight-line (linear) formulas.
Decision variableA quantity the model solves for.
Binary variableA variable that is only 0 or 1.
ConstraintA rule that every acceptable answer must follow.
ObjectiveThe score the model tries to make as low (or high) as possible.
Feasible / infeasibleFeasible: some answer follows every rule. Infeasible: the rules contradict each other.
GapA half-hour block nobody can cover.
ShortfallHow many hours someone is below their ideal.
Min-max trickAdd a variable that must be at least as big as everyone’s value, then minimize it, which improves the worst case.
Big-MA large constant that switches a rule on or off using a yes/no variable.
GurobiThe software that solves the optimization problem.
